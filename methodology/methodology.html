Nashville's Highland Rim Forest · Methodology

How we counted.

We read 32 Metro documents from 1973 to 2026 and pulled every statement on forests, trees, open space and steep slopes. Here is how 1,272 statements became 19 recommendations, made 216 times, and how we checked what Metro actually did.

From documents to a scorecard, in six steps

  • 1. We gathered Metro's documents. Adopted plans, studies and reports from 1973 to 2026, plus the laws, code and minutes that show what Metro actually did.
  • 2. We read them and copied out every statement. Every statement about forests, trees, open space and steep slopes, word for word, with its page number.
  • 3. We kept the ones that ask Metro to act. Recommendations, commitments, targets and cost estimates for the forest, found in 15 documents.
  • 4. We grouped the ones that ask for the same thing. The same request in different words counts once as a recommendation and each time as a mention.
  • 5. We looked for what was actually done. Ordinances, code, executive orders, budgets and minutes: proof of action, not more plans.
  • 6. We scored each recommendation. 1 fully implemented, 15 carried out in part, 3 with no implementation reported.

What we reviewed

We collected Metro Nashville's adopted plans, studies and reports that address forests, trees, open space or steep slopes, from the 1973 Natural Environmental Analysis to the 2026 Plan to Play update. To see what was done, not only what was planned, we added the ordinances, code sections, executive orders, resolutions, minutes, agendas and releases that record action.

In all we read 54 files making up 37 documents. A document can span several files: NashvilleNext is one general plan published in 17 files, and the 2026 Plan to Play update is two (plan and appendix). We count documents, not files.

Counted and not counted

32 documents are counted. Six more were read but left out of every number: two drafts never adopted (Livable Nashville 2017, Stormwater Master Plan 2025), two advisory committee reports (Green Ribbon Committee 2009, Sustainability Advisory Committee 2021), one background report written for NashvilleNext (2013), and one ordinance that was never enacted (BL2006-1046). They show what was proposed, not what Metro adopted. They appear in the list below, shaded.

How we pulled the statements

  • Every statement on forests, trees, open space or steep slopes was copied word for word into a workbook, with the document and page number. That gave 1,272 statements.
  • The three oldest documents (1973, 1981, 1992) survive only as scans. We converted them to text and checked each quoted statement against the page image.
  • Each statement was given a type (recommendation, commitment, target, funding estimate, baseline, definition and others) and a topic.

What counts as a recommendation

Recommendation
A statement that asks Metro to do something: a recommendation, a commitment, a target or a funding estimate. Metro's plans make recommendations; we do not call them promises.
Forest recommendation
A recommendation about the forest: its topic is forest, or it matches one of the 19 recurring recommendations. 340 statements qualify, in 15 documents (10 plans and 5 studies and reports).
The 19 recurring recommendations
Many recommendations ask for the same thing in different words, across decades. We grouped those into 19 recommendations. Together they were made 216 times, in 13 documents, from 1973 to 2026. Each time a document makes one counts once.

How we scored what was done

For each of the 19 recommendations we looked for evidence of action: an enacted ordinance, code in force, an executive order, money allocated, or a recorded decision. Six enacted rules turned up: two ordinances (BL2017-725 and BL2021-829), Executive Order 41, and three Metro Code sections (17.28.030, 17.28.065 and 17.40.440). Plans that restate a recommendation do not count as action.

  • Fully implemented: the action is in place and running. 1 of 19: a funded urban forestry program with current canopy studies.
  • Carried out in part: some action, short of what was recommended. 15 of 19.
  • No implementation reported: no evidence of action found. 3 of 19.

Rules we held to

  • Every figure traces to a quoted statement and a page, or to a law or record we can link.
  • Publication dates are given as month and year, or year where the document gives no month.
  • A second reviewer checked the workbook against the sources, and errors found were corrected.
  • Numbers are produced by a script run over the workbook, so they can be rerun and checked.

Limits

  • We reviewed what Metro has posted online plus three archived scans. Adopted plans that NashvilleNext replaced in 2015, such as the 2003 to 2012 community plans, are not online; records requests are pending.
  • Grouping recommendations takes judgment. The workbook shows every statement and the group it was assigned to, so anyone can check it.
  • "No implementation reported" means we found no record of action, not proof that none happened.
